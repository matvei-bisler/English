Part one

Four exercises

Not test questions. Four ways of taking the same skill apart — a ladder of claims, an audit of headlines, a paragraph reassembled, and a knockout bracket of answer choices. Work these first.

1

The Ladder

Every claim sits somewhere on a ladder. The bottom rung is what anyone can see. Each rung above it adds something: more places, more often, a purpose, a mind. The higher a claim sits, the more evidence it needs. Most arguments go wrong when someone climbs two rungs and does not notice.

1.1

Put the six claims on the ladder, from rung 1 (weakest, needs the least evidence) to rung 6 (strongest).

Click a claim, then click a rung. Click a placed claim to take it back.

1.2

Each rung adds exactly one thing to the rung below it. Match the step to what it adds.

1.3

Here is what has actually been published. Draw the line across the ladder where the evidence stops.

Click the gap between two rungs to lay the red line there.

1.4

In each pair, one sentence sits higher on the ladder. Mark it, and click the word that puts it there.

The decisive word may be in either sentence — including the lower one.

1.5

Which choice most logically completes the text?

2

The Audit

One study, six headlines. Your job is the job of a fact-checker: decide what each headline is doing to the study behind it.

The study · written for this exercise

A team surveyed 2,400 adults in one country. Each person reported how many hours a week they spent on hobbies with other people, and filled in a standard questionnaire on loneliness. People who reported more hours on shared hobbies had lower loneliness scores. The pattern was the same for men and women. The survey was carried out once, in March. The researchers note that they cannot tell which came first.

2.1

Give each headline one verdict.

2.2

In each headline that goes beyond the data, click the one word that carries the problem.

Leave a headline untouched if it does not go beyond the data. Click a marked word again to clear it.

2.3

H5 is the hardest one. Which change would make it accurate?

H5 — Shared hobbies may protect against loneliness, researchers suggest

2.4

Put these four verbs in order, from the weakest claim to the strongest.

2.5

Which choice most accurately completes the sentence?

3

The Assembly

Seven sentences from one paragraph, shuffled. One extra sentence has been mixed in that does not belong anywhere.

3.1

Put seven of the cards in order. Leave one card out.

Click a card, then click a numbered slot. The last slot is for the card that does not belong.

3.2

Each card carries a signal that fixes where it goes. Match the card to its signal.

3.3

The extra card is on topic and it is true. Where does it look as though it fits, and what goes wrong if you put it there?

3.4

Which sentence would best follow H?

H — The last animal in that herd died in 1627.

4

The Bracket

Four answer choices, knocked out in pairs. In each match you pick a winner and give the loser a reason for losing.

Bracket 1 · text

In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute chose seven unsolved problems in mathematics and offered a million dollars for the solution of each one. The Institute said its aim was not to start a competition but to record problems that mathematicians had struggled with for a long time. Only one of the seven has been solved since. The other six are still open, and the list has had an effect its authors did not plan: it has made a small number of problems far better known than the rest of the subject.

4.1

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Play the bracket. Semi-final 1: A against C. Semi-final 2: B against D. Then the final.

4.2

Give each of the three losing choices one reason from the list.

4.3

Now the question changes: which choice best describes what the Institute did in 2000?

Same four choices, new question. Which one wins this bracket?

Bracket 2 · text

Some questions are easy to check but seem hard to solve. If someone hands you a finished sudoku, you can check it in a minute; filling in a large one from an empty grid may take hours. The P versus NP problem asks whether that difference is real, or whether we have simply not found the fast method yet. Much of modern encryption depends on the difference being real.

4.4

Which choice best describes the function of the sudoku example?

Play the bracket. Semi-final 1: A against C. Semi-final 2: B against D. Then the final.

4.5

Give each of the three losing choices one reason from the list.

4.6

Choice D is a true statement about the text. Click the sentence it is a correct description of.

D — It explains why the problem matters for encryption.
